# 1. Purpose

This rubric defines the complete pilot evaluation logic for employees and the manager.

It is the authoritative source for:

- Fixed employee criteria.
- Definitions and boundaries.
- Behavioral Anchors from 1 to 5.
- Evidence and observation guidance.
- Practical examples and anti-examples.
- Section and criterion weights.
- Project Contribution rating guidance.
- Upward manager evaluation.
- Bias controls.
- Calibration and versioning.

It does not authorize AI to assign, recommend, or approve performance ratings.

---

# 2. Research Basis

This rubric was adapted from official performance-management, AI-risk, AI-skills, software-engineering, and team-effectiveness guidance.

The sources were used as design inputs, not copied as a ready-made template.

Key adopted principles include:

- Performance standards should be clear, understandable, individual, and linked to meaningful work.
- Performance management includes planning, monitoring, feedback, development, and periodic evaluation.
- AI work should be evaluated through professional capability rather than attachment to one technology.
- AI work requires explicit attention to mapping, measurement, governance, risk, reliability, and accountability.
- Team effectiveness and manager quality require clarity, psychological safety, trust, support, and useful feedback.

The pilot context remains decisive: a flat AI team, high autonomy, AI-assisted implementation, project and workstream ownership, research, experimentation, and human final judgment.

---

# 3. Non-Negotiable Rubric Principles

1. Employee and manager use the same criterion definitions and anchors.
2. Rating 3 means consistent achievement of the expected standard.
3. Ratings describe the complete evaluation period, not one event.
4. AI does not suggest a rating.
5. Documentation Readiness is not Performance Score.
6. Raw activity volume is not performance.
7. Project count, commit count, changed lines, and task count are not performance.
8. Research consumption alone is not applied learning.
9. Failed experiments may demonstrate strong performance when methodical and decision-producing.
10. Workplace behavior must be work-related and observable, not a personality preference.
11. Approved leave and external blockers must not be treated as performance failure.
12. Shared work must not automatically become equal individual credit.
13. The same work must not be counted multiple times without a distinct evaluation purpose.
14. Dynamic criteria must not be applied retroactively.
15. Rating 5 requires sustained wider impact, not perfection or one exceptional result.
16. The manager makes the final rating decision.

# 4. Rating Scale

| Rating | Meaning |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Significantly below expectations |
| 2 | Below expectations in important areas |
| 3 | Consistently meets expectations |
| 4 | Consistently exceeds expectations |
| 5 | Exceptional, sustained, and clearly impactful performance |

## 4.1 Rating Interpretation

**Rating 1** reflects a serious or recurring pattern that materially affects work, team, reliability, or outcomes.

**Rating 2** reflects inconsistent achievement of important expectations, even when some acceptable work exists.

**Rating 3** is the normal successful rating. It means the employee reliably performs what the role and context require.

**Rating 4** requires repeated value beyond normal expectations, not simply completing more work.

**Rating 5** requires sustained impact beyond individual task completion, such as improving several projects, strengthening team capability, creating reusable methods, or materially changing product or technical direction.

# 7. Criterion Records

## PPB-01 — Responsibility and Reliability

**Section:** Professional Performance and Workplace Behavior  
**Assessment basis:** Observation-Based + Outcome-Based + Project Records  
**Internal weight:** 30%

### Definition

Consistently owns assigned responsibilities, keeps work visible, follows through on commitments, raises blockers early, and protects continuity through clear handover and status management.

### Why It Matters

The pilot team works with high autonomy and limited task-level supervision. Reliable ownership is necessary for projects to continue without constant managerial intervention.

### Included

Follow-through; timely status communication; early blocker escalation; realistic commitments; continuity during leave or reassignment; accountability for decisions within the employee’s responsibility window.

### Excluded

Raw hours worked; always being available; accepting unreasonable workload; project outcomes controlled mainly by clients, data access, or external dependencies; technical quality assessed elsewhere.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Repeatedly leaves responsibilities unclear or unattended, misses commitments without early notice, and requires frequent intervention to restore visibility or continuity. |
| 2 | Shows partial ownership but follow-through, status clarity, or blocker escalation is inconsistent and sometimes causes avoidable delay or confusion. |
| 3 | Reliably owns assigned work, communicates status and blockers in time, follows through on commitments, and completes appropriate handovers. |
| 4 | Anticipates continuity risks, improves visibility for others, resolves ownership gaps early, and repeatedly makes delivery more dependable beyond their own tasks. |
| 5 | Creates a sustained standard of ownership that materially improves team reliability, prevents recurring operational failure, and strengthens continuity across multiple projects or workstreams. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Manager observation; Thursday check-ins; project/workstream status history; handovers; responsibility windows; delivery records; documented blocker escalation.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Keeps project status current, raises a data dependency before it blocks the team, and completes a usable handover before leave.
- Rating 3: Revises an expected completion date with a clear reason and next step rather than allowing the date to become silently outdated.
- Rating 4: Detects that ownership between two workstreams is unclear and coordinates a documented resolution before integration is affected.
- Rating 5: Establishes a repeatable continuity practice that is adopted across the department and measurably reduces stalled or ownerless work.
- Anti-example: Working late or responding instantly does not by itself demonstrate reliability if commitments, status, and handovers remain unclear.

## PPB-02 — Collaboration and Knowledge Sharing

**Section:** Professional Performance and Workplace Behavior  
**Assessment basis:** Observation-Based + Peer/Project Records + Mixed  
**Internal weight:** 25%

### Definition

Contributes constructively to shared work, supports colleagues, represents contribution fairly, and transfers useful knowledge so the team can progress without unnecessary dependency on one person.

### Why It Matters

Projects and workstreams are collaborative, responsibilities may overlap, and knowledge must survive delegation, leave, and changing ownership.

### Included

Practical support; constructive review; shared problem solving; fair attribution; knowledge transfer; usable explanations; participation in joint decisions.

### Excluded

Popularity; sociability; being talkative; doing another employee’s work permanently; technical documentation quality assessed under EED-04.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Repeatedly withholds needed context, creates avoidable friction, misrepresents shared contribution, or makes collaboration materially harder. |
| 2 | Cooperates when directed but support, knowledge sharing, or contribution clarity is inconsistent and others often need to reconstruct missing context. |
| 3 | Works constructively with colleagues, shares relevant knowledge, supports joint work, and represents individual and team contributions fairly. |
| 4 | Proactively removes collaboration barriers, improves shared understanding, and repeatedly helps others become more capable or independent. |
| 5 | Creates sustained team-wide capability through exceptional knowledge transfer, fair collaboration practices, and reusable methods that materially improve collective performance. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Manager observation; shared updates; peer acknowledgment; review comments; handover quality; learning sessions; contribution records; reusable guides.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Reviews a colleague’s approach, explains a risk clearly, and records the joint decision without claiming sole credit.
- Rating 3: Shares a tested setup or lesson that allows another contributor to continue the work.
- Rating 4: Converts repeated individual questions into a reusable team guide and helps colleagues apply it.
- Rating 5: Builds a sustained knowledge-sharing practice that reduces dependency on individuals across several projects.
- Anti-example: Attending meetings or frequently commenting does not by itself demonstrate meaningful collaboration.

## PPB-03 — Communication and Professional Conduct

**Section:** Professional Performance and Workplace Behavior  
**Assessment basis:** Observation-Based  
**Internal weight:** 20%

### Definition

Communicates work-relevant information clearly, respectfully, and at the appropriate time; handles disagreement and feedback professionally; and makes risks, decisions, and expectations understandable to relevant people.

### Why It Matters

The manager is not expected to direct every technical task, so clarity and professional exchange are essential for informed decisions and coordination.

### Included

Clear explanation; timely communication; respectful disagreement; active listening; appropriate feedback; accurate representation of uncertainty and risk.

### Excluded

Extroversion; friendliness as personality; smiling; avoiding all disagreement; presentation polish unrelated to work; durable technical documentation assessed under EED-04.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Communication is repeatedly unclear, late, disrespectful, or misleading, causing avoidable misunderstanding, conflict, or poor decisions. |
| 2 | Communicates basic information but clarity, timing, listening, or professional handling of disagreement is inconsistent in important situations. |
| 3 | Communicates clearly and respectfully, raises relevant information in time, listens to others, and handles feedback and disagreement professionally. |
| 4 | Adapts communication effectively to technical and non-technical audiences, surfaces difficult issues constructively, and repeatedly improves shared decisions. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional clarity and trust across complex situations, enabling difficult decisions, resolving recurring communication failure, and strengthening professional team norms. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Manager observation; meeting outcomes; decision records; stakeholder communication; discussion notes; feedback interactions.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Explains why a model result is preliminary and separates facts from assumptions before a decision is made.
- Rating 3: Disagrees with an approach respectfully and provides a clear alternative.
- Rating 4: Translates a complex technical risk into an actionable decision for a non-technical stakeholder.
- Rating 5: Establishes a communication approach that materially improves alignment across the department and external stakeholders.
- Anti-example: Being agreeable or avoiding conflict is not automatically strong professional communication.

## PPB-04 — Initiative and Adaptability

**Section:** Professional Performance and Workplace Behavior  
**Assessment basis:** Observation-Based + Outcome-Based  
**Internal weight:** 25%

### Definition

Moves work forward without waiting for detailed technical instructions, identifies useful next steps, and adapts direction responsibly when priorities, evidence, or constraints change.

### Why It Matters

The department operates through broad product and POC direction rather than detailed task assignment, and AI technology changes rapidly.

### Included

Self-directed next steps; useful proposals; responsible experimentation; adapting to new evidence; reprioritization; recovery from changed assumptions.

### Excluded

Uncoordinated activity; changing direction without reason; research quality assessed in Section 2; solution quality assessed in Section 3.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frequently waits for detailed direction, continues an unsuitable path despite clear signals, or initiates uncoordinated work that creates avoidable disruption. |
| 2 | Shows initiative in familiar situations but often needs prompting to define next steps or adapt when priorities and evidence change. |
| 3 | Independently identifies appropriate next steps, proposes useful options, and adapts responsibly to changing priorities or information. |
| 4 | Repeatedly anticipates useful opportunities or risks, redirects work with sound reasoning, and helps projects adapt faster without losing control. |
| 5 | Consistently turns ambiguity and change into high-value direction that materially improves multiple projects, team methods, or product opportunities. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Manager observation; proposed approaches; project decisions; priority changes; coaching history; project/workstream timelines.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Receives a broad POC objective, identifies the next research and implementation steps, and adjusts after early test results.
- Rating 3: Changes a plan when a dependency fails and documents the reason and new path.
- Rating 4: Anticipates a likely integration problem and validates an alternative before it blocks delivery.
- Rating 5: Identifies and develops a new reusable product capability that becomes valuable across several client contexts.
- Anti-example: Starting many ideas without finishing, validating, or coordinating them is not strong initiative.

## ARL-01 — Problem and Hypothesis Formulation

**Section:** AI Research, Learning, and Development  
**Assessment basis:** Artifact-Based + Mixed  
**Internal weight:** 20%

### Definition

Transforms an unclear need into a well-framed technical or research problem with explicit objectives, assumptions, constraints, and testable questions.

### Why It Matters

AI work often begins with ambiguous product or client direction. Poor framing produces wasted research, misleading experiments, and unsuitable solutions.

### Included

Problem definition; user or business context; hypothesis; assumptions; constraints; success question; uncertainty identification.

### Excluded

Full project success criteria; final architecture; experiment execution; generic restatement of a client request.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Regularly begins work without a clear problem, objective, or testable question, leading to irrelevant research or results that cannot support a decision. |
| 2 | Identifies the general problem but important assumptions, constraints, or hypotheses remain unclear and require substantial correction. |
| 3 | Frames the problem adequately, identifies relevant assumptions and constraints, and defines questions that can guide research or testing. |
| 4 | Repeatedly clarifies complex ambiguity, exposes hidden assumptions, and produces strong hypotheses that improve the efficiency and relevance of downstream work. |
| 5 | Creates exceptionally clear and reusable problem-framing approaches that materially improve decisions across multiple projects or workstreams. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Project/workstream documents; research questions; hypothesis records; decision notes; experiment plans; clarification updates.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Defines whether memory personalization should improve retrieval relevance and identifies privacy and latency as constraints.
- Rating 3: Separates a client’s desired outcome from an assumed technical solution before selecting models.
- Rating 4: Detects that a weak OCR result is actually caused by image quality distribution and reframes the experiment accordingly.
- Rating 5: Creates a reusable framing method that consistently prevents solution-first errors across the department.
- Anti-example: Rewriting the project description in technical language is not sufficient if the uncertainty remains undefined.

## ARL-02 — Research Quality and Technical Exploration

**Section:** AI Research, Learning, and Development  
**Assessment basis:** Artifact-Based + Outcome-Based  
**Internal weight:** 25%

### Definition

Selects, studies, and compares relevant sources, tools, models, and approaches with sufficient depth and focus to inform the work.

### Why It Matters

Rapid AI change requires continuous exploration, but value comes from relevant and critical research rather than consuming large amounts of content.

### Included

Source selection; relevance; breadth and depth; comparison; credibility; understanding limitations; targeted exploration.

### Excluded

Number of papers, videos, repositories, or courses; experiment quality assessed under ARL-03; implementation assessed under Section 3.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Research is repeatedly superficial, poorly targeted, unreliable, or disconnected from the problem, leading to weak or misleading direction. |
| 2 | Finds relevant material but comparison, critical understanding, or focus is inconsistent and important alternatives or limitations are often missed. |
| 3 | Uses appropriate and credible sources, explores relevant alternatives, and extracts information that meaningfully informs the work. |
| 4 | Conducts focused, critical, and efficient exploration that repeatedly reveals important options, limitations, or opportunities others might miss. |
| 5 | Produces exceptional research synthesis that materially shifts product or technical direction and becomes a durable reference for multiple projects or the team. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Research notes; source lists; comparison documents; repository reviews; paper summaries; technical recommendations; applied learning records.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Compares several memory approaches using relevant project constraints and documents meaningful trade-offs.
- Rating 3: Reviews a research paper and an implementation repository, identifying where the published assumptions do not match the current data.
- Rating 4: Finds an alternative model or method that materially reduces cost or operational complexity after a focused comparison.
- Rating 5: Creates a research synthesis that becomes the department’s reference and informs several product decisions.
- Anti-example: Listing many links or completing a course does not demonstrate research quality without understanding and relevance.

## ARL-03 — Experiment and Evaluation Design

**Section:** AI Research, Learning, and Development  
**Assessment basis:** Artifact-Based + Outcome-Based  
**Internal weight:** 25%

### Definition

Designs fair, reproducible, and decision-relevant experiments with suitable baselines, test cases, measures, controls, and documented conditions.

### Why It Matters

AI results are easy to overstate when comparisons are inconsistent, samples are weak, or metrics do not match the real use case.

### Included

Baseline; test set; metric choice; qualitative and quantitative evaluation; reproducibility; controls; failure cases; evaluation plan.

### Excluded

Final production reliability; implementation quality; merely running a model; client acceptance unless included in dynamic criteria.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Experiments repeatedly lack valid comparison, appropriate measures, or documented conditions, making results unreliable or unusable for decisions. |
| 2 | Runs useful tests but design quality is inconsistent; baselines, samples, metrics, or controls are incomplete in important cases. |
| 3 | Designs appropriate experiments with clear baselines, measures, test conditions, and enough documentation to support a practical decision. |
| 4 | Repeatedly builds robust evaluations that expose limitations, reduce false conclusions, and improve confidence in difficult technical choices. |
| 5 | Establishes exceptional, reusable evaluation methods that materially raise the quality and reliability of experimentation across the department. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Experiment plans; benchmarks; test datasets; baseline results; evaluation tables; failure analysis; reproducibility instructions.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Compares two OCR models on the same representative sample with speed and accuracy measures.
- Rating 3: Defines a baseline and test cases before evaluating an agent-memory approach.
- Rating 4: Adds failure-case categories and confidence analysis that change the recommended solution.
- Rating 5: Builds a reusable evaluation framework adopted across multiple AI products.
- Anti-example: Showing one successful demo or cherry-picked output is not a sound experiment.

## ARL-04 — Insight, Decision-Making, and Applied Learning

**Section:** AI Research, Learning, and Development  
**Assessment basis:** Outcome-Based + Mixed  
**Internal weight:** 30%

### Definition

Interprets research and experimental results accurately, converts them into justified decisions or practical improvements, and transfers useful learning when appropriate.

### Why It Matters

Research and learning create value only when they improve understanding, choices, work methods, products, or team capability.

### Included

Interpretation; conclusion; decision rationale; learning application; rejected-path value; knowledge transfer; uncertainty communication.

### Excluded

Raw research volume; architecture execution; project outcome itself; confident claims unsupported by results.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frequently draws unsupported conclusions, fails to extract usable learning, or repeats unproductive paths without adapting decisions. |
| 2 | Identifies basic lessons but conclusions, application, or decision rationale is inconsistent and often needs significant guidance. |
| 3 | Interprets results appropriately, records practical conclusions, applies learning to decisions or work, and shares relevant knowledge. |
| 4 | Repeatedly converts ambiguous or failed results into high-value decisions, improvements, and transferable learning beyond the immediate task. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional, sustained decision quality and reusable insight that materially changes products, methods, or team capability across multiple contexts. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Decision records; recommendations; applied changes; lessons learned; rejected-option rationale; knowledge sessions; coaching insights.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Concludes that a model is unsuitable for weak images, records why, and chooses a better next experiment.
- Rating 3: Applies learning from a course to improve an actual workflow and documents the effect.
- Rating 4: Uses a failed POC to identify a reusable constraint that prevents repeated waste in later projects.
- Rating 5: Produces insight that changes the department’s product strategy or operating method across several projects.
- Anti-example: Stating that something 'worked' or 'did not work' without interpreting why is not applied learning.

## EED-01 — Solution Design and Technical Judgment

**Section:** Engineering, Execution, and Documentation  
**Assessment basis:** Artifact-Based + Outcome-Based + Mixed  
**Internal weight:** 25%

### Definition

Selects and structures a solution appropriate to the problem, constraints, risk, and expected future use, with clear boundaries, dependencies, and justified trade-offs.

### Why It Matters

AI-assisted development makes code generation easier, but sound architecture and technical judgment remain essential.

### Included

Architecture; component boundaries; technology selection; simplicity; modularity; risk trade-offs; dependency design; technical decision rationale.

### Excluded

Research depth; experiment method; code volume; final project success; theoretical complexity not required by the use case.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frequently selects unsuitable or unnecessarily complex designs, overlooks major dependencies or constraints, and requires substantial redesign. |
| 2 | Produces workable designs in simple cases but important trade-offs, boundaries, or future implications are inconsistently handled. |
| 3 | Designs solutions appropriate to the problem, explains key choices, manages dependencies, and balances simplicity with maintainability. |
| 4 | Repeatedly makes strong technical judgments in complex contexts, avoids unnecessary complexity, and improves architecture beyond immediate requirements. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional design direction or reusable architecture that materially improves multiple products, reliability, or future development capability. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Architecture diagrams; technical decision records; project/workstream documents; design reviews; dependency maps; implementation outcomes.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Chooses a simple modular workflow that fits the POC while leaving clear extension points.
- Rating 3: Documents why a local or external model is suitable for the project’s constraints.
- Rating 4: Simplifies an overengineered design while preserving future maintainability and reducing operational risk.
- Rating 5: Creates a reusable architecture pattern adopted across multiple products.
- Anti-example: Using more services, frameworks, or agents does not automatically demonstrate better design.

## EED-02 — Execution Quality and Oversight of Agent Outputs

**Section:** Engineering, Execution, and Documentation  
**Assessment basis:** Artifact-Based + Outcome-Based  
**Internal weight:** 30%

### Definition

Turns approved direction into correct, maintainable work and actively reviews, tests, and improves outputs produced manually or by AI coding agents.

### Why It Matters

The team relies heavily on coding agents. Professional quality depends on direction, review, correction, and ownership—not on who typed the code.

### Included

Implementation correctness; maintainability; code and configuration organization; agent prompting and control; review; error detection; iterative correction; responsibility for output.

### Excluded

Commit count; manual coding speed; tool preference; experimentation quality; final integration reliability assessed under EED-03.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frequently accepts incorrect or unsuitable outputs, produces fragile work, or cannot explain and take responsibility for what was implemented. |
| 2 | Produces usable work but review, correction, maintainability, or agent oversight is inconsistent and recurring defects require substantial support. |
| 3 | Implements correctly, reviews agent outputs, understands the solution, corrects issues, and leaves work maintainable for its intended context. |
| 4 | Repeatedly delivers high-quality execution, catches subtle agent or implementation errors, and improves methods that increase speed without sacrificing control. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional execution practices or reusable agent-assisted methods that materially improve quality and delivery capability across the team. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Code and configuration; PRs; reviews; defect history; agent-generated changes with correction records; implementation demos; maintainability outcomes.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Uses Codex to build a feature, reviews the code, corrects unsafe assumptions, tests it, and can explain the implementation.
- Rating 3: Refactors an agent-generated component to match project architecture and maintainability needs.
- Rating 4: Detects a subtle data or security issue in generated output and improves the review method for future work.
- Rating 5: Develops a reusable agent-assisted engineering workflow that materially improves quality across the department.
- Anti-example: Generating a large amount of code quickly is not strong execution if the employee cannot validate or maintain it.

## EED-03 — Testing, Reliability, and Integration

**Section:** Engineering, Execution, and Documentation  
**Assessment basis:** Artifact-Based + Outcome-Based  
**Internal weight:** 25%

### Definition

Verifies that components work as intended, handles relevant failure conditions, and integrates them reliably with the surrounding system or delivery environment.

### Why It Matters

A successful isolated demo is not sufficient for a dependable POC, client solution, or product.

### Included

Functional tests; integration tests; failure cases; observability; error handling; environment readiness; reproducible operation; integration validation.

### Excluded

Research experiment methodology; general code quality; client acceptance criteria unless dynamically assigned; perfection or zero-defect expectation.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Frequently delivers unverified or poorly integrated work, overlooks basic failure conditions, and causes repeated instability or unusable results. |
| 2 | Performs basic testing but coverage of important integration, reliability, or failure scenarios is inconsistent and issues are often discovered late. |
| 3 | Tests relevant behavior, verifies integration, handles expected failure conditions, and delivers a suitably reliable result for the project stage. |
| 4 | Repeatedly anticipates difficult integration and reliability risks, improves validation depth, and prevents issues that would materially affect use or delivery. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional, reusable reliability or integration practices that materially improve several products or the department’s delivery confidence. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Test results; CI logs; integration demos; error-handling records; deployment checks; bug and resolution history; monitoring outputs.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Tests normal and expected failure paths and validates the component against the actual integration interface.
- Rating 3: Documents and verifies environment requirements so another employee can reproduce the result.
- Rating 4: Builds automated integration checks that prevent repeated regressions across workstreams.
- Rating 5: Establishes a reusable testing or reliability framework adopted across multiple products.
- Anti-example: A demo that succeeds once in a controlled environment is not sufficient evidence of reliability.

## EED-04 — Documentation and Reproducibility

**Section:** Engineering, Execution, and Documentation

**Internal weight:** 20%

### Definition

Maintains durable, accurate, and usable documentation that allows others to understand decisions, operate the work, reproduce important results, and continue development.

### Why It Matters

The system depends on continuity, delegation, shared ownership, and AI-assisted work that must remain understandable beyond the original contributor.

### Included

Project/workstream documents; setup and operation; architecture; decision rationale; experiment reproduction; update quality as a sustained practice; handover usability.

### Excluded

A single update completeness score; writing volume; polished formatting without usable content; communication in meetings.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Documentation is repeatedly absent, outdated, or unusable, leaving key work dependent on the employee’s memory and making continuation or reproduction difficult. |
| 2 | Documents basic information but important decisions, operation steps, or current state are inconsistently maintained and others often need clarification. |
| 3 | Maintains documentation that accurately supports understanding, operation, reproduction, and continuation of assigned work. |
| 4 | Repeatedly creates clear, efficient, and reusable documentation that reduces dependency, improves onboarding, and strengthens project continuity. |
| 5 | Establishes exceptional documentation and reproducibility practices that materially improve knowledge continuity across multiple projects or the department. |

### Evidence and Observation Guidance

Project/workstream documents; setup guides; architecture diagrams; decision records; handovers; reproducibility results; document version history.

### Examples and Anti-Example

- Rating 3: Keeps the Workstream document current and provides enough setup and decision context for a delegate to continue.
- Rating 3: Records why a model was rejected and how the benchmark can be rerun.
- Rating 4: Converts fragmented project knowledge into a concise reusable structure that materially reduces handover time.
- Rating 5: Creates a documentation approach adopted across the department and measurably improves continuity and reproducibility.
- Anti-example: A high Documentation Readiness percentage on several updates does not automatically justify a high performance rating.

# 8. Project Contribution — 25%

## 8.1 Purpose

Project Contribution evaluates what the employee actually contributed within the context of active projects and workstreams.

It considers:

- Project-level dynamic criteria.
- Workstream-level dynamic criteria.
- Responsibility windows.
- Primary, Acting, or Contributor role.
- Individual and shared contribution.
- Evidence and results.
- Complexity and uncertainty.
- External dependencies.
- Scope changes.
- Attribution disputes.
- Work completed during delegation.

## 8.2 No Automatic Average

The system must not calculate Project Contribution from:

- Number of projects.
- Average project scores.
- Average workstream scores.
- Number of dynamic criteria.
- Number of updates.
- Evidence count.
- Commit count.

The employee submits one self-rating.

The manager issues one final section rating.

## 8.3 Project Contribution Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Contribution is repeatedly unclear, substantially incomplete, or below the agreed project/workstream expectations within the employee’s responsibility periods. |
| 2 | Some meaningful contribution exists, but important agreed expectations, ownership responsibilities, or delivery quality are inconsistent or require substantial support. |
| 3 | Consistently makes the expected contribution across assigned projects and workstreams, appropriately meeting active criteria within the actual context and responsibility period. |
| 4 | Repeatedly contributes beyond agreed expectations through strong ownership, high-value decisions, difficult problem resolution, or additional impact across assigned work. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional, sustained project impact that materially advances products, clients, reusable capabilities, or department methods beyond normal expectations. |

## 8.4 Required Context Review

Before assigning the rating, the manager must review:

- Employee responsibility periods.
- Agreed project and workstream criteria active during those periods.
- Work completed individually and collectively.
- Actual employee control over outcomes.
- External and organizational blockers.
- Approved leave and delegation.
- Project stage: research, POC, product, integration, or delivery.
- Attribution status.
- Material decisions and results.
- Whether the employee created durable value beyond visible output.

## 8.5 Examples

- Rating 3: Reliably fulfills the expected contribution across assigned workstreams, meets the active criteria, and maintains clear responsibility and evidence.
- Rating 4: Repeatedly exceeds agreed expectations through difficult problem resolution, strong ownership, integration impact, or valuable decisions beyond normal delivery.
- Rating 5: Creates sustained project impact that materially advances products, reusable capability, major client outcomes, or department methods across more than one context.
- Anti-example: Completing many small POCs does not automatically justify a higher rating than making a strong contribution to one long-term product.
- Anti-example: Being Primary Owner does not automatically justify more credit than Contributors.

# 9. Overlap and Double-Counting Rules

| Potential Overlap | Approved Boundary |
|---|---|
| Initiative vs Problem Formulation | Initiative measures movement and ownership; formulation measures quality of understanding and framing |
| Research vs Project Contribution | Research measures capability and method; Project Contribution measures contribution in actual project context |
| Applied Learning vs Engineering Execution | Applied learning measures insight and decision; execution measures implementation quality |
| Technical Judgment vs Experiment Design | Experiment design measures validity of evaluation; technical judgment measures solution and architecture choices |
| Documentation Criterion vs Documentation Readiness | Criterion measures sustained professional practice; readiness measures completeness of individual records |
| Collaboration vs Shared Project Credit | Collaboration measures team behavior; Project Contribution measures actual contribution |
| Communication vs Documentation | Communication is timely professional exchange; documentation is durable reproducibility |
| Responsibility vs Project Outcome | Responsibility measures ownership behavior; outcome may depend on external context |

## 9.1 Classification Rule

A single work event may support more than one criterion only when each criterion evaluates a distinct dimension.

Example:

- Designing a valid benchmark may support ARL-03.
- Using its result to select an architecture may support ARL-04 or EED-01.
- Delivering the agreed workstream outcome may support Project Contribution.

The same fact must not be repeated as three separate achievements without explaining the distinct dimension.

# 10. Evidence and Observation Rules

## 10.1 Appropriate Sources

- Direct artifact.
- Outcome.
- Project or workstream record.
- GitHub source.
- Test or benchmark.
- Decision record.
- Applied learning record.
- Manager observation.
- Peer acknowledgment.
- Self-report.
- Disputed attribution.

## 10.2 Interpretation Rules

- Evidence supports judgment but does not automatically set the rating.
- Observation-based criteria do not require artificial file evidence.
- Absence of evidence does not prove absence of behavior.
- Team evidence does not automatically become individual credit.
- Multiple weak artifacts do not outweigh one meaningful result.
- Evidence generated after the period must be marked.
- AI confidence describes claim support, not employee quality.
- A disputed record remains disputed even if the manager considers it during evaluation.

## 10.3 Manager Observation Standard

Manager observation must:

- Describe work-related behavior.
- Prefer repeated patterns over one event.
- Avoid personality preference.
- Identify impact or relevance.
- Allow employee clarification during discussion.

# 11. Assessment Workflow

## 11.1 Shared Rubric

Employee and manager use:

- The same criterion.
- The same definition.
- The same anchors.
- The same evaluation period.
- The same frozen rubric version.

## 11.2 Employee Self-Assessment

For every criterion, the employee provides:

- Rating.
- Justification.
- Selected evidence or context.
- Strengths.
- Development area when relevant.

## 11.3 Manager Assessment

The manager completes an independent initial assessment before viewing the employee’s rating.

For every criterion, the manager provides:

- Rating.
- Justification.
- Evidence, result, or observation basis.
- Strengths.
- Development area when relevant.

## 11.4 AI Assistance Sequence

Approved sequence:

1. Show criterion, definition, anchors, and relevant source material.
2. User chooses the rating.
3. AI may help structure a justification consistent with the selected sources.
4. User reviews, edits, and approves the justification.
5. AI does not change the rating or label it correct or incorrect.

## 11.5 Comparison and Discussion

The system highlights:

- Rating differences.
- Different evidence.
- Different interpretation of contribution.
- Different interpretation of consistency or impact.
- Missing rationale.
- Disputed attribution.

The discussion is for clarification and review.

The manager issues the final rating.

# 12. Upward Manager Evaluation

## 12.1 Framework

The pilot uses five manager criteria.

The manager rubric is separate from the employee technical rubric.

## MGR-01 — Direction and Priority Clarity

### Definition

Provides understandable direction, translates wider goals into useful priorities, and helps the team distinguish what matters now from what can wait.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Direction and priorities are repeatedly unclear or contradictory, causing substantial confusion, rework, or unmanaged switching. |
| 2 | Provides some direction, but priorities or expectations are often unclear, late, or changed without enough context. |
| 3 | Usually provides clear direction and priorities, explains material changes, and helps the team understand expected outcomes. |
| 4 | Consistently creates strong clarity in ambiguous situations, aligns competing priorities, and reduces avoidable rework. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional strategic clarity that materially improves team focus, product direction, and decision quality over time. |

### Comment Prompt

What repeated management behavior helped or reduced clarity and focus? Give a specific work-related example when useful.

---

## MGR-02 — Support, Barrier Removal, and Team Protection

### Definition

Enables employees to work effectively by removing organizational blockers, obtaining needed decisions or access, and protecting the team from avoidable disruption.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Regularly leaves major barriers unresolved, adds avoidable disruption, or fails to support the team in critical situations. |
| 2 | Provides support inconsistently; important blockers often remain too long or escalation occurs only after significant impact. |
| 3 | Provides appropriate support, escalates meaningful blockers, and protects the team from unnecessary operational disruption. |
| 4 | Anticipates and removes significant barriers early, manages stakeholders effectively, and repeatedly improves team operating conditions. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional organizational leverage that materially increases the team’s ability to deliver, experiment, and sustain high-quality work. |

### Comment Prompt

What repeated behavior helped remove or created barriers for the team? Explain the effect on your work or the team.

---

## MGR-03 — Communication, Listening, and Psychological Safety

### Definition

Communicates openly, listens seriously, invites professional disagreement, and maintains an environment where employees can raise risk, failure, uncertainty, and concerns without inappropriate retaliation.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Communication discourages honesty or disagreement, important concerns are dismissed, or employees reasonably fear negative consequences for speaking openly. |
| 2 | Listens and communicates in routine situations but reacts inconsistently to challenge, failure, or difficult feedback. |
| 3 | Communicates respectfully, listens to concerns, accepts professional disagreement, and allows employees to discuss mistakes and uncertainty. |
| 4 | Actively creates trust, responds constructively to difficult feedback, and repeatedly improves the quality and openness of team discussion. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional psychological safety and honest dialogue that materially improves learning, risk detection, and team decision-making. |

### Comment Prompt

How safe and useful is communication with the manager over time? Explain what behavior should continue or change.

---

## MGR-04 — Delegation, Fairness, and Recognition

### Definition

Delegates with appropriate trust, applies expectations fairly, gives credit accurately, and avoids unnecessary micromanagement.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Delegation, recognition, or treatment is repeatedly unfair, controlling, or inconsistent, materially damaging trust or ownership. |
| 2 | Delegates or recognizes contribution inconsistently; expectations or treatment sometimes appear unclear or uneven. |
| 3 | Delegates appropriately, treats employees fairly, recognizes meaningful contribution, and avoids unnecessary control. |
| 4 | Consistently strengthens ownership through trust, fair decisions, and accurate recognition while maintaining needed accountability. |
| 5 | Creates an exceptional culture of trust, fairness, and ownership that materially improves initiative, retention of knowledge, and team performance. |

### Comment Prompt

What repeated behavior affected trust, fairness, delegation, or recognition? Give the context needed to understand your rating.

---

## MGR-05 — Feedback, Development, and Innovation Support

### Definition

Provides useful feedback, supports employee growth, and creates room for research, experimentation, learning, and responsible innovation.

### Behavioral Anchors

| Rating | Anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Rarely provides useful feedback or development support and repeatedly discourages learning, experimentation, or employee growth. |
| 2 | Provides occasional feedback and support, but it is often late, general, or disconnected from practical development. |
| 3 | Provides timely, useful feedback, supports reasonable development actions, and allows responsible research and experimentation. |
| 4 | Consistently coaches employees, connects development to real work, and creates strong conditions for learning and innovation. |
| 5 | Creates exceptional sustained growth and innovation capability, materially raising employee development and the department’s ability to learn and adapt. |

### Comment Prompt

What repeated behavior supported or limited feedback, growth, learning, or innovation? Explain what would improve the experience.

## 12.2 Pilot Visibility Rules

The LeapAI AI Department pilot uses `Identified` upward manager evaluation.

- Employees evaluate the manager quarterly in Year 1.
- The manager sees the employee name, criterion ratings, comments, submission status, and timestamp.
- The manager sees which eligible employees have not submitted.
- Submitted responses may be viewed without waiting for every employee.
- Approved leave is shown clearly and does not block visibility of other submissions.
- Employees must be told before submission that the pilot is not anonymous.
- Retaliation for good-faith evaluation is prohibited.
- AI may summarize repeated themes, but the summary does not replace the identified responses.

## 12.3 Future Product Visibility Modes

The rubric and product may support:

1. `Identified`.
2. `Manager-Blinded`.
3. `Anonymous Aggregated`.

The selected mode is part of the cycle snapshot and cannot be changed during an active cycle.

Privacy-safe prompts, topic suppression, identity separation, and restricted original access apply only when the selected mode requires them.

## 12.4 Manager Development Output

The manager receives:

- Individual ratings and comments in the pilot.
- Completion status by employee.
- Criterion averages and distributions.
- Repeated strengths.
- Repeated improvement themes.
- Trend against prior cycles.
- Suggested leadership development actions.

The system does not label the manager successful or failed automatically.

# 13. Bias and Fairness Controls

| Bias | Warning Sign | Corrective Question |
|---|---|---|
| Recency bias | Recent work dominates the rating | What pattern appears across the full period? |
| Halo effect | One strength raises all ratings | What independent evidence supports this criterion? |
| Horn effect | One weakness lowers unrelated ratings | Is this behavior relevant to this criterion? |
| Visibility bias | Client-facing or vocal work appears more valuable | What less visible work created equivalent or greater value? |
| Similarity bias | Manager favors employees with similar style | Would the rating change if the same result came from a different personality? |
| Extroversion bias | Sociability is mistaken for collaboration | What observable team contribution occurred? |
| Project prestige bias | Large client project is rated above internal product work | What was the employee’s actual responsibility and impact? |
| Outcome bias | Final success hides poor method, or failure hides strong method | What was within the employee’s control and how sound was the process? |
| AI fluency bias | Fast agent use is mistaken for engineering quality | Did the employee direct, review, validate, and own the output? |
| Effort-impact confusion | Long hours are mistaken for effectiveness | What useful outcome, learning, or risk reduction resulted? |
| Team-credit inflation | Whole project success is copied to every member | What did this employee contribute during their responsibility period? |
| Leave bias | Approved absence reduces perceived reliability | Was the responsibility properly handed over and excluded from the period? |
| Documentation-volume bias | More records imply better performance | Does the documentation create clarity, continuity, or reproducibility? |
| One-incident bias | One mistake defines the rating | Is this a repeated pattern or an isolated event? |
| Self-promotion bias | Strong narration receives more credit | What do the records, outcomes, and observed behavior support? |

# 14. Weight Rationale

## 14.1 Professional Performance — 20%

A meaningful but not dominant weight.

The department requires professional ownership and collaboration, but the evaluation should remain strongly connected to AI capability, engineering, and projects.

## 14.2 AI Research and Learning — 25%

Research, experimentation, learning, and decision formation are central to the department’s operating model and rapid technology change.

## 14.3 Engineering and Execution — 30%

The department must convert exploration into reliable products, POCs, integrations, and usable systems.

AI-assisted generation increases the importance of technical judgment, review, testing, and maintainability.

## 14.4 Project Contribution — 25%

The section ensures that evaluation remains connected to actual responsibility and outcomes.

Its weight is fixed regardless of project count.

## 14.5 Pilot Adjustment

Weights are the recommended Version 1 pilot baseline.

The manager may create a future template version after reviewing a completed cycle.

No active-cycle change is permitted.

# 16. Calibration Process

## 16.1 Cycle 1 Calibration Status

The first completed employee and manager evaluation cycle is marked `Calibration — Non-Baseline`.

It is stored and reviewed, but it is not used as the official longitudinal performance baseline or for financial, promotion, disciplinary, or employment decisions.

Cycle 2 becomes the first official baseline unless a later approved policy states otherwise.

## 16.2 Before Cycle Launch

- Review definitions and anchors with employees.
- Confirm examples match current work.
- Confirm dynamic criteria are active before evaluation.
- Confirm weights total 100%.
- Freeze rubric version.

## 16.3 After Each Quarterly Cycle

Review:

- Criteria repeatedly misunderstood.
- Anchors that fail to distinguish ratings.
- Excessive concentration at one rating.
- Double-counted evidence.
- Criteria unrelated to actual work.
- Evaluation workload.
- Employee reservations.
- Manager feedback.
- Upward-evaluation anonymity risks.

## 16.4 Change Governance

- Active rubric remains frozen.
- Changes create a new version.
- New version applies to future cycles.
- Historical evaluations retain the original version.
- Major rubric changes must be disclosed in cross-cycle comparisons.
